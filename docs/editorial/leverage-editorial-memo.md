# Leverage: ten editorial improvements

Article: `content/agency/leverage/index.md`

These are the ten ranked recommendations from the editorial review. No alternative article draft is included. This is an editorial assessment of the existing text, not a literature review.

The article's strongest idea is worth retaining: **influence becomes more consequential when it changes the conditions under which decisions are made, rather than merely winning agreement in a meeting.**

The problem is not just the language. The article mixes a theory of power, negotiation advice, an execution checklist, and a campaign manual without resolving the tensions between them. It asks readers to identify evidence that would prove them wrong, but also to make reversal irrational. It treats legitimacy as a source of leverage, but advises them to “never break pretext.” These contradictions matter more than the grammatical roughness.

The editorial target is a measured analytical essay: precise claims, explicit qualifications, concrete mechanisms, and sustained prose—not academic-sounding vocabulary.

## 1. Make the tension between durable change and entrenchment the central argument

The article currently assumes that making an initiative difficult to reverse is evidence of success. Yet a poorly performing initiative can also become indispensable through budget commitments, technical dependencies, and political sponsorship.

**Improvement:** Distinguish durability earned through continuing value from durability manufactured through exit costs. This would connect the article's currently conflicting commitments to falsifiability and lock-in.

For someone advising a powerful leader, this is the most important question: **are we strengthening the institution's capacity to act, or protecting our decision from correction?**

## 2. Replace the framework inventory with a line of reasoning

“Strategic Reality Model,” “Wedge,” “First-Principles Execution Loop,” and the subsequent sections read as assembled management frameworks. The placeholders make the text resemble a working prompt rather than a finished article.

**Improvement:** Organise the essay around a sequence:

- What gives an actor leverage?
- What makes that leverage usable?
- How does it produce commitment and institutional change?
- What can go wrong?
- How should its use be assessed?

Keep checklists, if wanted, in a separate practitioner's appendix. Replace headings such as “Narrative Control (Legitimacy System)” with direct ones such as “Evidence, interpretation, and authority.”

## 3. Distinguish leverage from authority, persuasion, and implementation

The article sometimes uses *leverage* to mean any effective action. Removing unnecessary process steps can improve execution without changing anyone's bargaining position. Formal authority can secure a decision without ensuring cooperation.

**Improvement:** Define leverage narrowly enough to explain something: an advantage arising from another party's dependence, available alternatives, or exposure to consequences. Then explain how authority, expertise, trust, and operational capability contribute to that advantage.

This also prevents the article from attributing every successful organisational intervention to leverage.

## 4. Treat legitimacy as a constraint on action, not merely an instrument of influence

The original already recognises normative legitimacy, governance, and evidence standards. The blind spot is that it mostly treats them as resources supporting the initiative.

“Never break pretext” directly undermines this position: it suggests maintaining a stated justification that may differ from the actual purpose.

**Improvement:** Distinguish legitimate confidentiality from misleading explanations. Ask whether the action remains defensible to the relevant board, regulator, workforce, or public if its purpose and method become known. The applicable duties differ by institution; executives, public officials, and board members do not have interchangeable mandates.

## 5. Replace the two-party model with an account of multiple audiences

The counterparty map is useful, but a powerful person is rarely negotiating only for themselves. They may need an agreement that can survive scrutiny from their board, peers, staff, constituents, or successor.

**Improvement:** Separate personal preference, organisational interest, and formal mandate. Examine who can authorise an agreement, who must implement it, and who bears the consequences.

A private “yes” from a prominent sponsor may be less useful than a narrower agreement that the implementing organisation can actually honour.

## 6. Account for reciprocal dependence and the distribution of costs

The article asks what the other party needs, but gives less attention to what the initiator will need from them after securing agreement.

**Improvement:** Assess the continuing need for cooperation, expertise, candid reporting, and discretionary effort. Show who gains and who pays. A change can benefit the enterprise while imposing concentrated costs on one department; that department's resistance need not be irrational or self-serving.

This makes the discussion of incentives more substantial than a simple exchange of X for Y.

## 7. Distinguish obstruction from information that the proposal is wrong

“Deny, Delay, Dilute, Divert, Escalate” describes possible behaviour, not its cause. Escalation may be the correct exercise of oversight. Delay may reflect an unresolved dependency. Reduced scope may improve the proposal.

**Improvement:** Require competing explanations before labelling resistance. Extend falsifiability beyond the initial proposal to the interpretation of other people's behaviour.

For leaders surrounded by deference, the missing perspective is particularly important: pressure can make opposition less visible without making the underlying problem disappear.

## 8. Rework the treatment of uncertainty and negative leverage

Positive, negative, and uncertainty leverage overlap. Withholding a benefit can impose a cost; a deadline can both impose costs and change uncertainty.

**Improvement:** Present these as mechanisms, not a clean or exhaustive taxonomy. Distinguish reducing uncertainty through reliable commitments from exploiting uncertainty through ambiguity. Explain that consequences are useful only when credible, proportionate, and within the actor's authority.

Do not imply that seniority grants the right to conceal material information or create artificial urgency.

## 9. Measure results independently of compliance

Opt-out rates, adoption, dependencies, and reversal attempts can show that a new process has become established. They cannot establish that it deserves to remain.

**Improvement:** Separate implementation measures from outcome measures and costs. Pair adoption with service quality, total cost, risk, workload, and whatever substantive outcome justified the change. Treat low reported resistance cautiously when disagreement carries career costs.

Retain the article's commitment discipline, but ask whether the commitment is producing value—not only whether it is being fulfilled.

## 10. Use one worked example and make the intellectual references do real work

BATNA helps explain alternatives. Behavioural economics helps explain the role of defaults and friction. The Kotter mapping, by contrast, currently adds a second sequence without explaining why it is needed.

**Improvement:** Follow one explicitly hypothetical case through negotiation, implementation, resistance, and review. Retain named frameworks only where they clarify a mechanism; source and qualify them if the piece is prepared for journal publication.

A concrete example will expose gaps that polished abstractions can conceal.
